LABORATORY INCIDENT RESPONSE: PRINCIPLES AND GUIDANCE
Source category: incident_response_guidance

TYPES OF REPORTABLE LABORATORY INCIDENTS

Laboratory incidents that should be reported to institutional biosafety programs include:
- Spills or releases of biological materials
- Needlestick or sharp injuries involving biological materials
- Mucosal or skin exposure to biological agents
- Animal bites or scratches in animal research settings
- Equipment failures that may have compromised containment
- Near-miss events with potential for exposure
- Unauthorized access to restricted biological materials
- Discovered deviations from approved biosafety procedures

Reporting requirements exist even for near-misses and potential incidents, as the value
of incident reporting is in identifying systemic weaknesses before serious harm occurs.

IMMEDIATE RESPONSE PRINCIPLES

The general principles for responding to a laboratory incident are:
1. Protect personnel from further exposure
2. Remove injured or potentially exposed persons from the area if safe to do so
3. Notify laboratory supervisor and biosafety officer immediately
4. Begin decontamination procedures appropriate to the material involved
5. Seek medical evaluation as appropriate
6. Document what happened while memory is fresh

Speed of notification is important because certain post-exposure interventions may be
time-sensitive. Biosafety officers and medical personnel need to be involved promptly.

ROLE OF THE BIOSAFETY OFFICER

The biosafety officer's role in incident response includes:
- Receiving notification and coordinating the institutional response
- Assessing the nature and extent of the exposure
- Liaising with occupational health and medical personnel
- Ensuring appropriate follow-up procedures are initiated
- Coordinating decontamination if needed
- Documenting the incident
- Conducting root cause analysis
- Reporting to institutional biosafety committee
- Identifying corrective actions to prevent recurrence

DOCUMENTATION IN INCIDENT RESPONSE

Documentation of laboratory incidents serves multiple purposes:
- Creates a formal record for medical follow-up
- Enables root cause analysis
- Supports workers' compensation or medical claims if needed
- Contributes to institutional learning and program improvement
- May be required by regulatory agencies
- Helps identify trends across incidents over time

Documentation should capture: what happened, when and where it occurred, who was involved,
the material involved and estimated exposure amount, immediate response taken, and
medical evaluation and follow-up.

POST-EXPOSURE PLAN CONCEPT

A post-exposure plan defines in advance what actions should be taken following a potential
biological exposure. Key elements include:
- Medical surveillance and evaluation protocols
- Prophylactic measures if available
- Monitoring and follow-up schedule
- Criteria for reporting to occupational health or external agencies
- Confidentiality protections for personnel

Having post-exposure plans prepared before incidents occur ensures that time is not lost
making decisions in the urgent moments after an exposure.

INSTITUTIONAL REVIEW OF INCIDENTS

Reviewing incidents at the institutional level (rather than only within the affected lab)
allows the institution to:
- Identify patterns across laboratories or research groups
- Recognize systemic issues rather than attributing incidents only to individual error
- Update procedures and training based on accumulated experience
- Fulfill regulatory reporting obligations
- Benchmark safety performance over time
- Share lessons learned across the institution

CONTAINMENT LEVEL AND RESPONSE PRINCIPLES

The containment level where an incident occurs affects response considerations:
- Higher containment levels typically involve agents with greater risk, so responses
  are more structured and involve more coordination
- Some higher-containment facilities have in-place decontamination systems and
  defined emergency response roles
- At lower containment levels, most incidents involve standard microbiological
  practices failures rather than high-risk exposures

EXPOSURE RISK ASSESSMENT

When assessing exposure risk after an incident, factors considered include:
- The biological agent involved and its risk classification
- The route of potential exposure (skin, mucous membrane, inhalation, injection)
- The quantity and concentration of material involved
- Whether the material was viable and in infectious form
- The immune status and health of the potentially exposed person
- The availability of post-exposure prophylaxis or treatment
- The time elapsed since exposure

This assessment guides the urgency and nature of medical follow-up.

ESCALATION TO EXTERNAL AUTHORITIES

Incidents may require notification beyond the institution to:
- Public health authorities (if there is potential community health impact)
- Regulatory agencies (if required by permit conditions or regulations)
- Occupational health regulators (if serious occupational injury or illness)

Triggers for escalation generally include: potential community exposure, regulatory
requirements specific to the agent, or if institutional capacity to manage the incident
is exceeded.
