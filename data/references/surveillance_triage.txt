INFECTIOUS DISEASE SURVEILLANCE: PRINCIPLES AND CONCEPTS
Source category: surveillance_reporting_guidance

TYPES OF SURVEILLANCE SYSTEMS

Case-based surveillance: Relies on reports of confirmed or probable cases meeting a
defined case definition. Each case is individually reported through a notification chain
from clinician to local public health to regional/national authorities. Provides detailed
information but subject to underreporting and delays.

Syndromic surveillance: Monitors health data streams (emergency department visits,
pharmacy sales, school absenteeism, etc.) for patterns that may indicate disease activity
before formal diagnosis. Can detect unusual disease clustering early but has low specificity
and generates false alarms. Supplements but does not replace case-based surveillance.

Sentinel surveillance: Uses a defined network of reporting sites (clinicians, hospitals,
labs) to monitor disease trends in a representative sample. More feasible than universal
surveillance for conditions where comprehensive reporting is impractical.

Active vs passive surveillance:
- Passive: relies on health providers to report cases voluntarily. Low cost but underreports.
- Active: public health authorities systematically contact health providers to collect data.
  More complete but resource-intensive.

REPORTABLE DISEASE CRITERIA

Diseases are generally designated reportable based on:
- Public health significance: severity, transmissibility, treatability, preventability
- Potential for outbreak
- Availability of effective public health interventions
- International reporting obligations (International Health Regulations)
- Legal or regulatory requirements

Clinicians are required by law in most jurisdictions to report notifiable conditions to
local or state/national health authorities within specified time frames. Time frames vary:
immediate notification for suspected serious outbreaks, within 24-72 hours for urgent
conditions, and weekly for less urgent reportable diseases.

OUTBREAK DEFINITION AND THRESHOLD

An outbreak is generally defined as an occurrence of cases of disease in excess of what
would normally be expected in that community, region, or season. Key concepts:

Endemic baseline: The expected background level of disease in a population. Determined
by historical data.

Epidemic threshold: The level of disease incidence above which an outbreak investigation
should be initiated. Often defined statistically (e.g., two standard deviations above
baseline) or operationally (e.g., a defined number of unexpected cases in a defined period).

Cluster: A group of cases of a disease in time and/or place that is greater than expected.
A cluster may or may not constitute an outbreak depending on the background rate.

SIGNAL DETECTION PRINCIPLES

A surveillance signal is an observed change in disease patterns that may indicate a public
health event worth investigating. Signal detection involves:

- Comparison to baseline: Is the observed rate above the expected endemic rate?
- Spatiotemporal clustering: Are cases concentrated in time or location?
- Severity or unusual presentation: Are cases more severe than typical?
- Common exposure: Do cases share an exposure suggesting a point source?
- Laboratory confirmation: Do cases have confirmed pathogen identity?

Not all signals represent true outbreaks. Many reflect reporting artifacts, changes in
case definitions, improved detection, or random variation. Signal verification is the
process of distinguishing true events from artifacts.

DATA QUALITY IN SURVEILLANCE

Common data quality issues in surveillance include:
- Underreporting: many cases are never diagnosed or reported
- Reporting delays: lag between illness onset, diagnosis, and notification
- Case definition inconsistency: different interpretations of case criteria
- Missing data on key variables (exposures, demographics, outcomes)
- Duplicate records
- Selection bias in sentinel systems

These issues affect the completeness and timeliness of surveillance signals.

SURVEILLANCE DATA FLOW

Data typically flows: Clinician/laboratory → Local health department → State/provincial
health authority → National health authority → International reporting (WHO, etc.)

Each level of the system may add delay but also validation, aggregation, and analysis.
Electronic reporting systems have reduced delays significantly in many settings.
Laboratory data flows in parallel with clinical reports and may be the primary notification
for some conditions.

TRIAGE OF UNUSUAL REPORTS

When multiple unusual reports arrive simultaneously, prioritization may consider:
- Severity and potential for mortality
- Potential for person-to-person transmission
- Size of the potentially exposed population
- Availability of effective intervention
- Unusual features suggesting a novel or deliberate event
- Geographic or temporal clustering suggesting a common source

INDICATORS OF NOVEL EVENTS

Features in surveillance data that may suggest a novel or unusual biological event:
- Disease pattern inconsistent with known endemic conditions for the season and location
- Unusual severity or clinical presentation
- Demographic pattern not consistent with typical disease (affects age groups that are
  usually spared)
- Rapid geographic spread inconsistent with known transmission rates
- Clustering in time suggesting a point-source exposure event
- Laboratory findings that do not match known pathogens
- Failure of normal clinical management approaches
