BIOSAFETY EDUCATION: PRINCIPLES AND CONCEPTS
Source category: biosafety_education_resources

BIOSAFETY LEVEL FRAMEWORK

Biosafety levels (BSL) are a set of containment precautions required to isolate dangerous
biological agents in an enclosed facility. The levels are graded from 1 (lowest risk) to
4 (highest risk), based on the agent's risk group classification.

BSL-1: Work with agents not known to consistently cause disease in healthy adults. Standard
microbiological practices are followed. No special primary or secondary barriers required
beyond a sink for handwashing.

BSL-2: Work with agents associated with human disease of moderate potential hazard to
personnel and the environment. All BSL-1 practices plus limited access, biohazard warning
signs, "sharps" precautions, and a defined biosafety manual. Biosafety cabinet (BSC) used
for potential aerosol-generating procedures.

BSL-3: Work with indigenous or exotic agents with potential for aerosol transmission
and that may cause serious or potentially lethal disease. All BSL-2 practices plus
controlled access, decontamination of all waste, laboratory clothes not worn outside lab,
baseline serum samples for personnel, and use of BSC for all manipulations.

BSL-4: Work with dangerous and exotic agents that pose a high individual risk of aerosol-
transmitted infections and for which no vaccine or therapy is available. Maximum containment.
All BSL-3 practices plus full-body, air-supplied positive pressure personnel suit OR class
III BSC.

RISK ASSESSMENT IN BIOSAFETY

A biological risk assessment considers:
- Agent characteristics: risk group, virulence, infective dose, route of transmission,
  stability, host range, availability of treatment or prophylaxis
- Laboratory procedures to be performed: potential for aerosol generation, sharps use,
  large volumes, work with high concentrations
- Personnel factors: training, experience, immune status, potential vulnerability
- Facility features: existing containment equipment, access controls, decontamination
  capability

The outcome of risk assessment determines the appropriate biosafety level and additional
controls required.

STANDARD MICROBIOLOGICAL PRACTICES

Standard microbiological practices are the baseline safety requirements applicable in all
biological laboratories. They include:
- Access limited to authorized personnel
- No eating, drinking, smoking, or application of cosmetics in the laboratory
- Prohibition of mouth pipetting
- Handwashing before leaving the laboratory
- Minimizing aerosol generation
- Decontaminating work surfaces after completing procedures
- Proper disposal of contaminated materials
- Personal protective equipment appropriate to the task

HIERARCHY OF CONTROLS IN BIOSAFETY

From most to least preferred:
1. Elimination - remove the hazard entirely
2. Substitution - replace with less hazardous agent or procedure
3. Engineering controls - biosafety cabinets, ventilation, physical containment
4. Administrative controls - standard operating procedures, training, restricted access
5. Personal protective equipment - the last line of defense

BIOSAFETY CABINETS

Biosafety cabinets provide a primary containment barrier:
- Class I: Provides personnel and environmental protection but not product protection;
  inward airflow only
- Class II (Types A1, A2, B1, B2): Provides personnel, environmental, and product
  protection; HEPA-filtered supply and exhaust air; most commonly used in biosafety
  work; Type B2 is fully ducted to outside
- Class III: Fully enclosed, gas-tight cabinet with glove ports; provides highest level
  of personnel and environmental protection; used in BSL-4

SAFETY CULTURE

A positive safety culture is characterized by:
- Shared values and commitment to safety across all levels
- Open communication about safety concerns without fear of punishment
- Leadership that models and reinforces safe behavior
- Learning from incidents and near-misses
- Ongoing training and competency assessment
- Recognition that safety rules are based on real risks, not bureaucratic requirements

INSTITUTIONAL BIOSAFETY COMMITTEES

An Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC) is responsible for:
- Reviewing and approving research involving biological hazards
- Ensuring compliance with applicable regulations and guidelines
- Reviewing biosafety procedures and accident reports
- Establishing emergency procedures
- Serving as the institutional authority for recombinant DNA and biological research
- Providing oversight of biosafety training programs

PPE SELECTION PRINCIPLES

PPE selection follows the hierarchy of controls principle - PPE is used in conjunction
with, not as a substitute for, other controls. Selection criteria include:
- The specific hazard and route of exposure
- The level of protection required based on risk assessment
- Task-specific requirements
- Compatibility with other PPE and work tasks
- Training requirements
- Limitations of the equipment

AEROSOL RISK IN BIOSAFETY

Aerosol generation is a key risk factor in biological work because:
- Infectious aerosols can reach the respiratory tract, which is an efficient infection route
- Aerosols are invisible and may persist in air
- Many common laboratory procedures generate aerosols (centrifugation, pipetting, vortexing)
- The potential for aerosol generation determines when a biosafety cabinet is required
  and at which containment level work should be performed
