HEALTH MISINFORMATION: TYPES, CAUSES, AND RESPONSE STRATEGIES
Source category: health_communication_misinformation

TYPES OF HEALTH MISINFORMATION

Health misinformation in biological health contexts typically falls into several categories:

1. Factual misinformation: Claims that are simply factually incorrect (e.g., incorrect
   statistics, misidentified pathogens, wrong symptoms attributed to a disease).

2. Context misinformation: Accurate information presented in a misleading context
   (e.g., real data from one outbreak used to characterize a different one).

3. Fabricated content: Wholly invented stories or documents falsely attributed to
   legitimate authorities.

4. Misleading content: Framing or presentation that creates false impressions even
   with accurate underlying facts.

5. Conspiracy theories: Claims that disease events are deliberately caused or concealed
   by powerful actors.

6. Exaggerated risk: Amplification of genuine risks beyond what evidence supports.

7. Minimized risk: Dismissal of genuine risks, often in opposition to official guidance.

FACT-CHECKING HEALTH CLAIMS

Fact-checkers evaluate health claims using:
- Primary source verification: checking original research papers, agency documents,
  institutional statements
- Expert consultation: seeking input from domain experts
- Contextual review: ensuring statistics and quotes are not taken out of context
- Identifying the original source and its credibility
- Checking whether a claim has been previously addressed
- Applying scientific consensus rather than minority viewpoints

For biological claims specifically, fact-checkers consider whether claims are consistent
with peer-reviewed epidemiological and clinical evidence.

PSYCHOLOGICAL FACTORS IN MISINFORMATION SUSCEPTIBILITY

People are more susceptible to health misinformation during biological health crises due to:
- Anxiety and threat perception: fear motivates information-seeking but also reduces
  critical evaluation
- Information overload: high volume of information makes verification difficult
- Confirmation bias: preferring information consistent with existing beliefs
- Distrust of institutions: prior negative experiences with health authorities
  increase openness to alternative explanations
- Social transmission: misinformation spreads through trusted social networks
- Proportionality heuristic: the magnitude of an event activates the belief that
  someone must have intended it

PREBUNKING (INOCULATION THEORY)

Prebunking is based on inoculation theory: exposing people to weakened forms of
misinformation techniques before they encounter real misinformation, building psychological
resistance.

Key elements:
- Explaining the manipulation techniques used in misinformation (e.g., cherry-picking
  evidence, false dichotomies, emotional appeals)
- Providing a refutation of the specific misinformation type before it is encountered
- Repeating inoculation at intervals

Evidence suggests prebunking is more effective than debunking after exposure because
it is harder to correct misinformation once believed than to prevent initial acceptance.

DEBUNKING STRATEGIES

When correcting misinformation after it has spread:
- Lead with the correct information, not the myth (the myth should not be the headline)
- Explain the correct version clearly before explaining why the myth is wrong
- Provide a plausible alternative explanation if the myth addressed a real concern
- Avoid repeating the misinformation in detail, which can inadvertently reinforce it
- Acknowledge the concerns underlying the misinformation

COUNTER-MESSAGING PRINCIPLES

Effective counter-messaging for biological health misinformation:
- Does not amplify the misinformation by extensively quoting it
- Provides context rather than only corrections
- Uses trusted messengers: local leaders, community health workers, peers
- Adapts to the specific beliefs and information ecosystem of the community
- Addresses underlying concerns, not just factual errors
- Is simple, memorable, and actionable

RESPONDING TO MEDIA ERRORS

Public health officials responding to media factual errors:
- Issue a clear, direct correction with the accurate information and its source
- Avoid being combative or personalizing the correction
- Use the correction as an opportunity to reinforce accurate messaging
- Brief journalists proactively to prevent errors rather than only correcting after the fact
- Provide resources for accurate information (official websites, data sources)

COMMUNITY HEALTH WORKERS AND LOCAL RUMOR MANAGEMENT

Community health workers address local health rumors by:
- Listening to rumors without immediate dismissal to understand the concern behind them
- Using community-level trusted messengers and communication channels
- Providing clear, simple explanations appropriate for the community's health literacy
- Following up to check whether the correction was accepted and understood
- Escalating persistent or harmful rumors to public health authorities for coordinated response

DESIGNING RESILIENT COMMUNICATIONS

Public health messages designed to be misinterpretation-resistant:
- Anticipate how messages can be misread or misused and pre-address likely misinterpretations
- Use plain language that leaves little room for misreading
- Include context and caveats rather than only headlines
- Avoid technical language that may be misunderstood by non-specialists
- Test messages with representative audiences before wide distribution
- Include clear calls to action so the purpose of the message is explicit
